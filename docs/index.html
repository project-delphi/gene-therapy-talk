ML in Gene Therapy

Engineering AAV when the search space is the problem

Ravi Kalia

Four Sequences That Worked

Four medicines. Each works because someone chose the sequence.

Vaccine 1: A Virus as Van

A chimp cold virus (adenovirus), emptied and reloaded.

  • Growth gene removed, spike gene added
  • It can no longer copy itself

≈35 kb of DNA (35,000 letters)

The virus is the courier, not the medicine.

The gene it carries was written, not discovered.

Source of the virus

The van: a courier, not the medicine

Voysey et al. (2021)

This is the Oxford/AstraZeneca vaccine. A vector is a virus turned into a delivery van: we delete its copying genes and load a gene we want cells to read. Why a chimp virus? Many people carry antibodies to human adenoviruses, which would stop the van. The van was off the shelf; only the sequence changed. Question: “Does it infect you?” No, it cannot copy itself. It only delivers the spike gene.

Vaccine 2: No Virus at All

Just the message, wrapped in a fat bubble.

  • mRNA with one building block swapped
  • Fat droplet carries it into cells

4,284 nucleotides

Designed in days from the virus genome.

Uğur ŞahinBioNTech

Özlem TüreciBioNTech

Polack et al. (2020)

Take the virus away and the sequence still works. Link to the central dogma: we hand the cell the mRNA, and ribosomes make spike. It never enters the nucleus or changes your DNA. The mRNA is fragile, so a lipid nanoparticle (a tiny fat bubble) carries it in. “Swapped how?” That is the next slide.

One Swap Calms the Cell

Your innate immune system treats plain mRNA as infection and destroys it.

Swapping every uridine for N1-methylpseudouridine quiets the alarm.

Same message, different chemistry: the alarm stays quiet.

Katalin Karikówith Drew Weissman — Nobel Prize, 2023

Polack et al. (2020)

Think of immune receptors as locks and chemical patterns on microbes as keys. Unmodified RNA looks viral, because human cells modify their own RNA. Karikó and Weissman kept the message and changed the feature the sensor looks for. The protein is identical. We meet this same trick again later, with CpG in DNA.

Muscular Dystrophy: A Gene Cut to Fit

Duchenne muscular dystrophy: muscle breaks down without dystrophin.

  • A virus (AAV) carries the gene to muscle
  • A shortened dystrophin
  • The full gene (11 kb) does not fit

≈5 kb cassette: gene plus switch

Calf swelling, from Duchenne’s 1868 plates.

The van is fixed. The cargo was redesigned.

Mendell et al. (2023)

AAV can hold only about 4.7 kb, but dystrophin is 11 kb. So the therapy uses a shortened version that keeps the key linking parts. A physical limit dictated a biological design, like a suitcase weight limit. It is a working compromise, not a full cure.

Cancer Antibody: The Odd One Out

No virus: a protein made in cultured cells.

  • Blocks PD-1, a brake on T cells
  • Mouse binding regions on a human frame

≈1.4 kb of coding sequence

Jimmy CarterAnnounced metastatic melanoma in August 2015; clear scans by December

Spread melanoma, treated with surgery, radiosurgery and this antibody.

The product is a protein. The asset is the sequence.

Emancipator (2020); Goldberg et al. (2016)

This is pembrolizumab (Keytruda). Some tumours press the PD-1 brake so T cells stop attacking. The antibody binds PD-1 like a key in a lock and releases the brake. It began in a mouse, so we graft its binding loops onto a human antibody. Did it cure Carter? He had three treatments at once, so his case alone cannot say. Trials show lasting responses.

Four Sequences, Same Scale

Length does not predict impact. The design problem is the same.

Let the blocks fill first. Each bead is one hundred bases. The vaccine vector is about twenty-five times longer than the antibody sequence, yet size predicts nothing. Choosing a sequence is a search problem, far too big to try exhaustively. That is the whole talk.

The Virus Worth Borrowing

AAV cannot copy itself alone. That defect is why we use it.

Success 1: Blindness

Problem. Without the RPE65 gene, light-sensing cells die.

AAV carries. A working RPE65 copy, injected under the retina. Luxturna, 2017.

Result. People could cross an obstacle course in dim light again.

First US-approved gene therapy for an inherited disease.

A retina in advanced degeneration. Photo: Christian Hamel, CC BY 2.0.

U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2026a)

A recessive loss-of-function disease: no working gene copy, so no working enzyme. Gene therapy adds a working copy. The photo shows general retinal degeneration, not specifically RPE65 disease. The eye is small and shielded, so a tiny dose stays put.

Student question: “Is it permanent?” Benefit has lasted years; lifetime is unknown.

Success 2: Weak Muscles

Problem. Without SMN1, the nerves that move muscles die.

AAV carries. A working SMN1 gene, in one drip into a vein. Zolgensma, 2019.

Result. Treated babies have sat up, and many have walked.

One drip, not lifelong treatment.

Nerve cells in the spinal cord drive muscles. Diagram: Walsh and Sved, CC BY-SA 4.0.

U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2026a)

The muscle is fine; the nerve telling it to move is dying. AAV9 can cross from blood into the nervous system. Treat early, because dead neurons are not replaced.

Student question: “Why a vein?” For babies, the blood route is simplest.

Success 3: Blood That Cannot Clot

Problem. In haemophilia B, the liver lacks clotting factor IX.

AAV carries. A working factor IX gene, in one drip. The liver makes the protein. Hemgenix, 2022.

Result. Bleeding fell and most people stopped regular injections.

The patient’s own liver becomes the factory.

Clotting-factor concentrate, the lifelong infusion gene therapy aims to replace. Photo: JJEv810, CC BY-SA 4.0.

U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2026a)

X-linked recessive, as in Mendelian genetics. We add an extra working copy; we do not repair the faulty gene. The vials are factor VIII (haemophilia A), shown only for the infusion burden.

Factor IX is small, 1.4 kb, so it fits in AAV’s 4.7 kb space. Remember this size limit; it returns.

Success 4: Deafness

Problem. Without OTOF, ear cells cannot signal to the brain.

AAV carries. A working OTOF gene, split across two viruses, into the cochlea. Otarmeni, 2026.

Result. Children who had never heard began to hear speech.

First approved treatment for genetic deafness.

The cochlea is the spiral in the inner ear. Diagram: Chittka and Brockmann, CC BY 2.5.

U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2026a)

The gene is about 6 kb, over the 4.7 kb limit. So two viruses each carry half, and the halves join inside the cell. Think of a long letter in two envelopes.

Student question: “Does it always work?” Less efficiently than one virus, since both halves must enter the same cell.

What Is a Virus?

A virus is instructions in a protein shell.

  • Shell: protects and sticks to a cell
  • Instructions: DNA or RNA
  • No machinery: it borrows yours

A virus is a delivery van carrying instructions.

One idea: a virus is a parcel of instructions in a shell, and it borrows our machinery. The shell is called the capsid. The bridge: keep the van, swap the instructions. That is gene therapy, and AAV is our van.

AAV Up Close

  • DNA genome of 4.7 kb
  • Shell (capsid) of 60 protein pieces
  • About 26 nm across
  • No known disease in humans

Drag it.

Xie et al. (2002)

A live 3D model of the real AAV2 shell. Turn it to the spikes: they bind the cell’s receptor, like lock and key. Everything we engineer later happens on the outside of this object.

Student question: “Does AAV make us ill?” No established disease, which is why it suits medicine.

Where the Symmetry Is

Sixty identical pieces, arranged like a 20-sided ball.

  • 5-fold ×6: a channel
  • 3-fold ×10: the spikes
  • 2-fold ×15: a dip

Xie et al. (2002)

Point at the three axis types. Five-fold: a pore the genome passes through. Three-fold: the spikes, where the virus binds cells and antibodies attach; most engineering happens here. Two-fold: a shallow dip. Message: not every place on the shell is equal.

From DNA to Protein

DNA
the stored copy

RNA
a working copy

Amino acids
one per codon

Protein
the folded machine

Same colours, same codons: DNA is read into RNA, RNA into amino acids, and the chain folds.

Central dogma in one picture. Follow one colour left to right: a three-base codon on the DNA is copied into RNA (transcription), the ribosome reads that RNA codon and adds one amino acid (translation), and the finished chain folds into a protein. Base counts are cut down for the slide. This is the route AAV’s genes take, so the next slide’s rep and cap are DNA that become proteins this way.

The Genome at a Glance

Two genes: rep copies and packages. cap builds the shell. Short DNA hairpins (ITRs) mark each end.

Samulski and Muzyczka (2014)

Do not read this out. Point at the ITRs: hairpin ends the cell uses as copy and packaging labels. Then rep and cap. A promoter is the on-switch before a gene, as in the lac operon.

Rep Proteins We Throw Away

Rep78 and Rep68

Start copying. They can also insert viral DNA at one spot on chromosome 19.

Rep52 and Rep40

Motors that pull the genome into the shell.

The numbers are sizes in kilodaltons.

Delete rep and the virus loses its fixed insertion site.

Kotin et al. (1990)

Alternative splicing, as students know: p5 gives Rep78 (unspliced) and Rep68 (spliced); p19 gives Rep52 and Rep40. Medical vectors drop rep. That avoids random insertion and cancer risk. The DNA floats free as a loop, so the effect fades where cells divide.

Student question: “Does the gene enter my chromosomes?” Mostly not, by design.

Three Shell Proteins

One gene, three versions.

VP1 87 kDa has a membrane-breaking tail
VP2 73 kDa not essential
VP3 62 kDa most of the shell

Ratio about 1 : 1 : 10.

Five VP1 copies let the virus escape the endosome.

Girod et al. (2002)

All three come from one gene with different start points. The cell swallows the virus into a bubble, the endosome. VP1’s hidden tail, an enzyme that cuts membranes, is exposed when the bubble turns acidic. That frees the virus. The ratio is 1:1:10, not 1:1:5.

Two Hidden Genes

AAP

Hidden inside cap. Helps build the shell.

MAAP

A different reading frame of the same DNA. Helps the virus leave the cell.

A 2019 mutation scan revealed MAAP.

A gene nobody had spotted emerged from the data.

Sonntag et al. (2010); Ogden et al. (2019)

DNA is read in triplets, so shifting one letter gives a different protein. Viruses overlap genes to save space. Ogden and colleagues mutated the shell gene letter by letter. The results did not fit one reading frame, and MAAP emerged. Hold this thought for mutational scanning later.

Defective by Design

AAV needs a helper virus to copy itself.

Adenovirus, a cold virus, can help.

Herpes simplex virus can too.

For medicine, this is a feature:

  • In the lab, helper genes come on a plasmid (a small DNA circle)
  • A vector that cannot copy cannot spread

Not copying itself is the safety feature.

Samulski and Muzyczka (2014)

The pivot of the section: dependence on a helper looks like a weakness but is the safety argument. AAV is a parasite of other viruses. The lab supplies helper genes on a plasmid instead.

Student question: “Could an adenovirus infection make it spread?” The vector carries no rep or cap, so no.

Why Not Adenovirus?

Good vaccine

  • Carries a larger gene
  • Strong gene expression
  • Strong immune response

Poor gene therapy

  • Immune system kills treated cells
  • Many people already have antibodies
  • Inflammation at therapeutic doses

A vaccine wants an immune response. Gene therapy does not.

Dunbar et al. (2018)

Seroprevalence: how many people carry antibodies to a virus. Memory means a fast, strong second response, and the vector is neutralised on arrival. One caveat: AAV is only relatively quiet. At high blood doses it is not silent. We return to this.

Serotypes: Same Shell, New Surface

Same fold, same 60 pieces. Only the outer loops differ.

  • AAV2 sticks to heparan sulfate
  • AAV9 sticks to galactose

Surface loops decide which tissue a virus reaches.

Xie et al. (2002); DiMattia et al. (2012); Summerford and Samulski (1998); Shen et al. (2011)

A serotype is a variant defined by antibody recognition. Tropism is which cells it enters: shell loops are the ligand, cell-surface sugars the receptor. Both shells share a fold, like two enzymes of one family. So we edit loops, not redesign a fold.

Student question: “Just pick the right serotype?” Often yes; engineering does better.

From Virus to Vector

A vector is a virus rebuilt as a delivery van. Every viral gene goes. Only the end “handles” stay.

Keep Only the End Handles

Gray et al. (2011); Zanta-Boussif et al. (2009)

The virus’s own genes are gone. What is left is a small cassette between two end sequences (ITRs), which the cell needs to copy and package the DNA. The cassette holds our therapeutic gene plus a promoter, the on-switch.

Everything must fit in one protein shell, the capsid, which holds only about 4.7 kb. Think of a suitcase of fixed size.

Size Decides the Medicine

Gene to deliver Size Fits?
Factor IX (clotting protein) 1.4 kb easily
micro-dystrophin (short muscle protein) 3.8 kb just
Factor VIII (trimmed clotting protein) 4.4 kb no room left
dystrophin (full length) 11 kb never

Duchenne therapy uses a shrunken “micro” dystrophin. Haemophilia A is harder to treat than B.

A shell’s size limit shapes the medicine.

Mendell et al. (2023)

Let the table do the work. Dystrophin’s gene is about 11 kb, over twice the suitcase, so the therapy delivers a shortened version. Factor IX is small, so haemophilia B got there first. Factor VIII leaves no room for a promoter.

Caveat: micro-dystrophin keeps the key parts but is not the full protein.

Read down the gene sizes: all but one are small, as the limit predicts. The number after AAV names the serotype, a shell variant that targets different tissues.

Otarmeni (2026) treats deafness from OTOF faults using two vectors, each with half the gene. The cost is a second vector and needing both halves in one cell.

Under the retina, in the brain and in the ear, the dose is small and stays put.

Getting In

Between injection and a working gene, most of the dose is lost.

From Needle to Cell

Mingozzi and High (2013)

Most of the dose is lost on the way. Many people already carry antibodies that block AAV, so trials screen for them, and a second dose usually fails. The liver and spleen mop up particles. The vessel wall is also a barrier.

The tapering band is illustrative; no single percentage is reliable. So doses must be huge.

The Journey In

Girod et al. (2002); Cataldi and McCarty (2010); Nakai et al. (2001)

This is the central dogma in motion. The virus binds a receptor, the cell takes it in, and many particles are digested in lysosomes (the red branch). Survivors reach the nucleus and the shell opens. The DNA becomes double-stranded, is transcribed, and the mRNA is translated into protein.

Press Play, or select a numbered stage to pause. The red branch is not a measured fraction. The route is simplified.

What Happens to the DNA

  • Cell copies the second strand
  • Repair enzymes join the ends into circles
  • Histones wrap the circles

The cell still notices this DNA.

Nakai et al. (2001)

Four phases: single strand, second strand, circles, then an episome, which is DNA persisting in the nucleus as a small circle. Wrapped in histones means it looks like your own DNA.

It is not invisible: the cell’s break-sensing proteins detect the hairpin ends. “Tolerated” is accurate; “undetected” is not.

Question: does it integrate? Mostly no.

From Hairpin to Chromatin

Nakai et al. (2001)

The still version of the animation. The strand arrives folded into hairpins. The cell builds the second strand and repair enzymes join the ends into circles. Histones assemble, and it is transcribed like any gene.

The DNA is read but never copied. Fine in non-dividing cells; in a growing liver, copies dilute.

Why Expression Fades

The circle is not copied when the cell divides.

Non-dividing cells (neurons, muscle): circles can last.

Dividing cells (a child’s liver): the vector is diluted.

The price of deleting rep, the gene that let the virus integrate.

Safety gained; permanence lost.

Lasting for decades is untested.

Nakai et al. (2001)

Link to DNA replication: the circle has no origin of replication the cell uses, so at mitosis copies are shared out and diluted. Neurons never divide, so nothing dilutes.

Do not promise lifelong expression. A first dose raises antibodies, so redosing usually fails.

Why delete rep? Integration carries its own safety risk.

Making It at Scale

One patient needs trillions of virus particles. That is a factory job.

Two Ways to Grow the Virus

Urabe et al. (2002)

Think of cell culture from a practical, just on a huge scale. A cell needs three sets of instructions: the gene with its end sequences, the virus’s own protein genes, and helper genes.

In human cells we transfect plasmids in. In insect cells, a second virus, baculovirus, carries them in.

Which gives cleaner batches? The evidence does not support a clear winner. Process and gene design matter more than cell type.

Henrietta Lacks: Where Cell Lines Began

1951, Baltimore. Henrietta Lacks, 31, is treated for cervical cancer. A doctor takes a tumour sample. Nobody asks her.

Her cells kept dividing in a dish. They became HeLa.

The first human cells that would not die.

Statue of Henrietta Lacks, Bristol. Photo: 14GTR, CC BY-SA 4.0.

A HeLa cell, scanning electron micrograph. Photo: NIH, public domain.

Our factory runs on cultured human cells, and cell lines start with her.

She was treated in 1951 and died that year. Her tumour cells did not die, so labs never ran out. HeLa helped test the polio vaccine.

Neither she nor her family were asked or told. That was legal then, and it shaped today’s consent rules.

Is AAV made in HeLa? No, it is HEK293, from embryonic kidney.

Purifying the Virus in Three Steps

Mingozzi and High (2013)

Like purifying a protein from a lysate: capture, polish, concentrate.

Capture uses beads that stick to the virus shell, like an antibody to its antigen. But the beads cannot tell full shells from empty ones.

Polishing removes the empty ones, using a small charge difference. Ultracentrifuges do this on the bench, but not at factory scale.

Why Empty Capsids Matter

An empty capsid is a shell with no gene. The body still sees it.

  • Foreign protein for immunity to attack
  • Uses up the patient’s tolerance
  • Counts towards the dose

Every batch is checked for full versus empty shells.

Foreign protein, no benefit.

Mingozzi and High (2013)

This bridges into immunology. The immune system reacts to the shell, and cannot tell full from empty.

So each empty capsid spends part of a limited budget and delivers nothing. A manufacturing quality sets a clinical ceiling.

The Dose Ceiling

A bigger dose works better, but harms more.

The Therapeutic Window

Manno et al. (2006); Hinderer et al. (2018)

This is the hinge of the talk. It is a dose-response curve: one line for benefit, one for harm.

The window between them is narrow. Low end: some patients carry antibodies and are excluded. High end: immune and liver damage.

We cannot move the harm curve much. We can shift benefit left with a better capsid. That is why capsid engineering matters.

Three Ways the Dose Fails

Before treatment

Neutralising antibodies block the virus. These patients cannot be treated.

At the right dose

Killer T cells destroy liver cells showing viral protein. Gene expression falls.

Above the right dose

Complement (blood proteins that attack foreign surfaces), plus liver and nerve damage.

Mingozzi and High (2013); Faust et al. (2013); Hinderer et al. (2018)

Walk left to right. Many people met natural AAV and made antibodies, which is immune memory. They block the vector from entering cells.

Middle: in the first haemophilia B trial, clotting Factor IX rose then fell. A T cell response to the capsid removed the treated liver cells.

Right: high doses activate complement and injure liver and nerves. These set today’s ceiling.

Engineering the Modules

We can redesign three parts of a gene therapy vector: the shell, the switch and the DNA ends.

Three Parts, Three Problems

Capsid

The protein shell. It picks the target cells and dodges antibodies.

A protein-surface problem.

Promoter

The DNA switch. It sets where, and how strongly, the gene turns on.

A gene-regulation problem.

ITR

Short DNA repeats at both ends. They help packaging and persistence.

A DNA-shape problem.

You know promoters from gene regulation; a capsid is the virus’s protein coat. ITRs are short repeats at each end of the vector’s DNA. They help pack it and keep it in the cell.

These are three different problems, so they need different methods. We spend longest on the capsid, which has the most data.

The Search Space Is Huge

The capsid protein has 735 amino acids. Each can be one of 20.

A 60-residue stretch alone has about 10⁷⁸ possible sequences.

Nine orange peaks are measured natural capsids. The rest is untested.

Romero and Arnold (2009)

Each point is a possible sequence. The nine orange peaks are natural AAV serotypes we have measured. Everything else is dark: nobody has tested it.

In practice we change a short stretch, say seven amino acids: 20 to the 7, about 1.3 billion variants. Each test needs virus made and injected into an animal, so we cannot test them all.

Capsid Shuffling

Parent genes are cut up and reassembled. Each colour is one parent.

Cut up several natural capsid genes. Let them reassemble as mosaics. Keep the ones that work.

Grimm et al. (2008)

This is directed evolution: make a diverse library of variants, put it in the target tissue, and keep what gets in. It is natural selection in a test tube.

Shuffling builds the library, like recombination in meiosis swapping blocks between parents. It finds combinations that one-mutation-at-a-time never would.

What Shuffling Gave Us

Grimm et al. (2008)

Shuffling gave useful tools, such as AAV-DJ. But we get one winner and no rule linking sequence to performance.

Change tissue and you must start the screen again. Machine learning aims to close that gap.

One Loop, a Billion Variants

It worked in mice. Mice were the wrong model.

Deverman et al. (2016)

Insert seven random amino acids into one surface loop, inject into mice, and recover virus from the brain. The winner, AAV-PHP.B, crossed the mouse blood-brain barrier far better.

It failed in primates, because it used a mouse protein, LY6A, that primates lack. The screen did its job; we picked the wrong animal.

What Is a Model?

Model: a rule learned from examples that predicts.

Training: adjusting the rule to fit the examples.

Testing: checking it on examples it has never seen.

A model takes an input, here a protein variant, and predicts an outcome. Nobody writes the rule; it is learned from data.

Training is like fitting a line to a scatter plot. Testing holds data back, so we know the model has not just memorised, like a student who memorises past papers.

How a Random Forest Predicts

Follow one variant down three trees. Do they agree?

Breiman (2001)

A decision tree is like a field-guide key: yes/no questions ending in an answer. Here the questions are about charge, bulk and so on.

Each tree sees different data, so they disagree a little. Averaging their answers cancels much of the error. The numbers on screen are illustrative.

From Mouse to Macaque

Fit4Function: one model per measurable property.

Six properties, combined.

88% of designed variants met all six.

No one predictor carries across species. All six together do.

Eid et al. (2024)

This answers the AAV-PHP.B problem. The team screened a library spread evenly, measured six functions, and trained one model for each.

Chart question: do predictions carry over to the macaque? Chance is 0.24. The best single predictor scores 0.86; all six together score 0.91.

Predict Fitness Before Measuring

Can 3D structure alone predict how well a capsid is produced?

No measured AAV data was used to train.

Threefold more mutations beat the natural capsid than random ones. Preprint.

A capsid you cannot make is no medicine.

All of this can be calculated from a structure.

Mikos et al. (2021)

Most work asks which tissue a capsid reaches. Few ask whether we can make enough of it.

Structure alone, such as how buried a residue is, tripled the hit rate. This is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed, so call it promising, not settled.

Score or Propose?

Ng and Jordan (2001)

A scoring model judges a variant you hand it, but it needs measured examples, so it guesses far from them. A generative model learns what working sequences look like, from sequences alone, then proposes new ones. Best pipeline: generate, score, then measure only the survivors in the lab.

Question: is it just copying? No, it learns the pattern, so samples are new but familiar.

What a VAE Does

A VAE squeezes, then rebuilds.

Encoder: sequence to a few numbers.

Decoder: numbers back to a sequence.

Move the marker: the sequence changes smoothly.

Ng and Jordan (2001); Bryant et al. (2021)

Like describing a face with a few numbers: width, eye spacing, nose length. The encoder does that for sequences; the decoder rebuilds one. Training keeps similar sequences close, so the map is smooth. Point at the strip: it changes gradually as the marker moves.

Question: do decoded sequences work? Not automatically. They are candidates to test.

Models That Design Proteins

A generative model learns patterns from many examples, then writes new ones that fit.

Like autocomplete for proteins.

The examples are sequences nature already made.

The model proposes new ones.

Data matters more than the type of model.

Bryant et al. (2021)

Autocomplete learns how sentences look. Here the sentences are protein sequences. You may hear VAE, GAN or diffusion: different pattern-learners, and you needn’t know them. AAV results improved because experiments gave enough measured examples, not because of a fancier model.

Question: is it guessing? It makes educated proposals; the experiment decides.

Mutate Everything, Measure Everything

Count variants before and after: winners rise, losers fall.

Ogden et al. (2019)

Change every position to every other amino acid, then test the whole library. Sequence it before and after. A variant’s sequence is its name tag: counts up means it works, counts down means it fails. Directed evolution keeps only survivors, like studying only marathon finishers. Models need the failures too.

Caveat: a rare variant can vanish by chance.

Letting Data Teach the Model

The model learns what works, instead of us assuming.

Ogden et al. (2019); Bryant et al. (2021)

Measured scores become training data. The model designs new versions, we measure them, and results feed the next round. Point at the graph: random changes quickly break the capsid, like typos in a manual. A model-designed library survives many more changes.

Caveat: we measure assembly at scale, not targeting or immune escape.

Promoters: Where a Gene Switches On

A promoter is DNA beside a gene where transcription starts.

Goal: gene on only in the target tissue, in as little DNA as possible.

Why:

  • wrong-tissue activity is unsafe
  • “on everywhere” can provoke immune attack

Specificity written into the DNA.

Gray et al. (2011)

You know promoters from transcription: where factors land and recruit RNA polymerase. Space in the virus is tiny, so a short promoter leaves more room for the gene. A liver-only promoter gives targeting at the level of gene activity, not delivery.

Question: why not just target delivery? You can, but the promoter adds a second layer.

Designed Promoters

Michael RobertsSynpromics → AskBio → Bayer · now Chromatin Bioscience

Screened libraries of short binding sites, then assembled the best into small, tissue-selective switches.

AMT-191: Fabry disease treatment. A liver-selective promoter drives the GLA gene.

First four patients: 27-208 times normal enzyme activity (company-reported, July 2025).

Designed, not borrowed.

uniQure (2025)

Fabry patients lack an enzyme, so a substrate builds up. Here the liver is switched on to supply it. The team built a compact liver-selective switch instead of borrowing a viral promoter.

Caveat: four patients, company data. It shows enzyme made, not clinical benefit or safety.

Why CpG Looks Like Danger

An innate alarm turns gene delivery into immune attack.

Faust et al. (2013); Mingozzi and High (2013)

Innate immunity spots patterns, not specific pathogens. Unmethylated CpG is one: rare in our DNA, common in bacteria and viruses. TLR9 detects it. Follow the cascade: interferon, dendritic cells, then T cells that kill the treated cells. So removing CpG removes the alarm.

Question: is our DNA never detected? Rarely; it is CpG-poor and methylated.

Editing Viral Ends Safely

The ITR is a short stretch at each end of the viral DNA. It folds into a hairpin that the enzyme Rep recognises.

It is also rich in CpG.

Only 145 bases.

Faust et al. (2013)

Watch where the CpG sites sit on the hairpin, and what changing bases does to its shape. The ITR works through its fold, like tRNA, so edits can break it. Rules: cut CpG, keep the fold, keep Rep able to bind.

Question: why not delete it? Without it the genome can’t be copied or packaged.

The limit is the dose ceiling: how much virus a patient can safely receive.

A better shell works at a lower dose.

  • designs that avoid immunity

Biology sets the rules; the rest is search.

Ogden et al. (2019); Eid et al. (2024)

The virus is safe because it can’t replicate alone and doesn’t integrate, but the dose must work first time. Immunity limits the dose, so each particle must do more. Machine learning helps because the design space is huge, and works because of experiments and biology.

Caveat: models predict assembly, not behaviour in human liver. That is the frontier.

Questions

Likely questions:

  • Why not redose? The antibody response to dose one blocks it. Plasmapheresis and IgG proteases are being tried.
  • Why not integrate deliberately? Insertional mutagenesis; and rAAV integration is largely non-targeted anyway. Nucleases and base editors are the other answer.
  • How real is DRG toxicity? Consistent across NHP studies, dose dependent, often subclinical but histologically unambiguous.
  • Does generative design beat directed evolution? On viability at distance, yes. On human tropism, not yet demonstrated.
  • Why is MAAP interesting? It is the clearest case of ML on sequence data revealing biology rather than just optimising it.
